七夕

Night of the Sevens

Four poems  ·  Tang & Song Dynasties

The Night of the Sevens – otherwise known as Double Seventh Festival – is sometimes called the Chinese version of Valentine’s Day, though I think it feels more ancient and folksy compared to the Western analogue. The holiday is rooted in a folktale of two star-crossed lovers – Cowherd Boy and Weaver Girl. You can read the full story here.

There are many variations to the story. I will quickly provide a short synthesis. The female lead is the Weaver Maiden, who is either a daughter or handmaiden in the Celestial Court – she belongs in the realm of the gods. On the other hand, the male lead is the Cowherd Boy, who is a kind-hearted, sincere, but impoverished orphan boy on earth. The Weaving Maiden weaves clouds and colors to adorn the sky while the humble Cowherd Boy unknowingly befriends a magical, deeply kind, and fatherly cow who helps him to meet the Maiden. They meet, marry, and have children together in the mortal realm. But their union offends the Heavenly Queen Mother who comes and kidnaps her daughter/maid when the Cowherd was out. The Cowherd gives chase and when he was just about to grab hold of his wife, the Heavenly Queen Mother slashes the heavens with her hairpin and creates a gap that would separate them forever – the Milky Way. Their sorrow and plight moved the living creatures of the earth, and a large train of magpies flew to the heavens to create a bridge across the Milky Way. Moved, the Queen Mother allowed them to meet once a year on the magpie bridge on the Seventh Day of the Seventh Month of the Lunar calendar – hence the name, Night of the Sevens. Traditionally, the festival focused on girls and women, and domestic skill. Girls took part in needlework competitions, displayed skill making small crafts or decorations and made wishes for skill in needlework.

There’s something I love about this folktale – I think it captures at its core something that deeply resonates with a sense of what ought to be or what truly matters and also the realities which can be cruel and cause suffering in this world. The beautiful, capable, lonely Maiden and a humble and kind-hearted young man who live a few years together treasuring what are perhaps the worthiest things in life. Then, reality interferes to create a barrier that even the most loyal love cannot cross. At the same time, one of the things that touched me about the story is that the living creatures of the earth were touched by their love and loyalty and sorrow and helped to build a bridge across the stars. It reminds me that perhaps the world around us is more alive than we realize and the recognition of what life truly is lingers about in unexpected places.

This is one of my favorite Double Seventh poems. It’s a Tang era shi poem that is beautifully constructed and full of double meanings. The poet wrote this poem on the Night of the Sevens in 853 after the loss of his beloved wife. Though, on another note, Li Shangyin was famed for his romantic and sensual poems, which point to the likelihood that he had other romantic encounters – so not sure how that fits in with his love for his wife!

《七夕》

“Night of the Sevens”

Li Shangyin (李商隐)  ·  Tang Dynasty, 853

鸾扇斜分凤幄开

Phoenix-plumed standards slant and part;1

phoenix-embroidered curtains open.

星桥横过鹊飞回

The magpies are flying home for this year

after bridging the Starry River.

争将世上无期别

Oh, how might I take the unending partings of this earthly realm

换得年年一度来

and exchange them for meeting again every year!2

  1. ↑ 鸾扇 (luán shàn) and 凤幄 (fèng wò) — the luan is a mythical phoenix-like bird; its feathers adorned ceremonial fans carried in royal and celestial processions. The phoenix-embroidered curtains complete the picture of a heavenly gathering, framing the Night of the Sevens as a grand celestial event.
  2. ↑ The closing couplet carries a deeply personal dimension: Li Shangyin’s wife Wang Shi had died shortly before he composed this poem. The “unending partings of this earthly realm” are his own — unlike the star-crossed lovers above, who at least have their annual reunion, his separation is permanent.

The poem below by Du Mu is a classic Double Seventh poem also from the Tang era in shi form. In contrast to Li Shangyin’s masterpiece above, this poem uses the occasion of the Night of the Seventh to portray a very different kind of life – a luxurious but deeply lonely life at the heart of the stratified palace system of the Tang era. The unmentioned heroine of this poem could be a palace attendant or a concubine or court lady – we do not know what her background or story is. What we do know is that faithful, exclusive love is quite rare in those places, where sex and courtship often involve unequal power dynamics, positions, politics, or simply the whims of higher-ranking men.

I think this poem excels at atmosphere and imagery – using temperature, color, light, texture, the combination of an inner room and outer palace grounds to exhibit and contrast beauty, elegance, luxury on one hand and loneliness, chilliness, bleakness on the other. Finally, our heroine lies down, bored, perhaps melancholically accepting, and gazes at the stars of the sincere lovers above. She is in her room alone and outside, the palace is unforgiving and cold.

《秋夕》

“Autumn Evening”

Du Mu (杜牧)  ·  Tang Dynasty, c. 9th century

银烛秋光冷画屏

The silver candlelight and waning autumn glow

chills the painted screen.

轻罗小扇扑流萤

She bats at fireflies

with a delicate silken fan.

天阶夜色凉如水

The night aspect

of palace terraces3

is chilly like water.

卧看牵牛织女星

She lies down and gazes at

the Cowherd and Weaving Maiden.4

  1. ↑ 天阶 (tiān jiē) — literally “heavenly stairs”; the stone terraces and walkways of the imperial palace complex. The word “heavenly” carries an irony here: these grand celestial-named steps are cold and empty rather than magnificent.
  2. ↑ 牵牛织女星 — the Cowherd Star (Altair, α Aquilae) and the Weaver Maiden Star (Vega, α Lyrae), the two bright stars separated by the Milky Way whose conjunction on the seventh night of the seventh month is the astronomical basis of the festival. The heroine gazes up at the one love story she cannot have.

The next poem by Qin Guan is another famous poem on the Night of the Seventh and its folktale. Written in the Song Dynasty in ci form, both form and tone contain the fluidity and emotional expression emblematic of the ci. Interestingly, Qin has a different take on true love – in his view, few moments of intense, transcendent love can be truer – or certainly no less true – than a lifetime of companionship across ordinary days. I’m not sure what I think about that. I still can’t help but feel that perhaps a lifetime of ordinary days is still the fullest and most satisfying fulfillment of that love. Honestly, I just don’t have the experience to tell!

《鵲桥仙 · 纤云弄巧》

“Wispy Clouds Weave Playfully”

Qin Guan (秦观)  ·  Song Dynasty, 1097

纤云弄巧，飞星传恨，银汉迢迢暗度。

Wispy clouds weave playfully;

shooting stars carry our painful yearning.

We silently cross the distant Milky River.

金风玉露一相逢，便胜却人间无数。

One meeting amid the golden autumnal wind

and amongst jeweled dew5 –

outweighs countless encounters on earth.

柔情似水，佳期如梦，忍顾鹊桥归路。

Tender ardor like water;

a blissful interlude like a dream.

Can I bear to glance back at the return path across the magpie bridge?

两情若是久长时，又岂在朝朝暮暮。

If two affectionate hearts long endure,

then surely, love cannot be measured only in the passing of ordinary days?6

  1. ↑ 金风玉露 (jīn fēng yù lù) — “golden wind and jade dew”: classical poetic terms for the cool, crystalline air of early autumn, the season in which the Night of the Sevens falls. The imagery makes the lovers’ single meeting feel precious and rare, like the season itself.
  2. ↑ The closing couplet (两情若是久长时，又岂在朝朝暮暮) is among the most quoted lines in Chinese poetry — a direct challenge to the idea that love requires constant daily presence. Qin Guan uses the lovers’ annual reunion to argue that depth of feeling matters more than duration of togetherness.

Li Qingzhao is probably China’s foremost female canonical poet. Her life was marked by a beautiful first love and marriage to her husband Zhao Mingcheng, with whom she shared scholarly pursuits and passions, progressing to long separation due to his official duties and then eventually war, exile, and widowhood due to the fall of the Northern Song, and a disastrous second marriage which ended in lawsuit and divorce. Her poems are famously…very sad. Even this poem, which was written before widowhood, carries the sense that she feels trapped under a very dark and heavy cloud which never lifts. Like many literary geniuses before and after her, I think this would probably qualify as (severe) depression today.

I think this poem – and likely many of her poems – are defined by a sense of utter abandonment and entrapment, best expressed in the line “barred and bolted a thousand times” below. Her pain is so deep that she can’t help but constantly ruminate on it – to the extent that even meeting her beloved cannot assuage her “parting grief.” In the end, she settles in a state of dull emotional turbulence as displayed in the last line. It is truly a beautiful piece of literary work, but it also describes truly a very distressed interior state. My heart goes out to her and I wish she were able to experience some relief and joy.

《行香子 · 七夕》

“Night of the Sevens”

Li Qingzhao (李清照)  ·  Song Dynasty, 1129 (uncertain)7

草际鸣蛩，惊落梧桐，

正人间、天上愁浓。

At the grass’s edge, crickets chirp

and startle the parasol leaves8

into falling.

Presently, grief hangs thick

on earth as in heaven.

云阶月地，关锁千重。

Cloud-pillowed stairs

on moonlit ground –

barred and bolted a thousand times.

纵浮槎来，浮槎去，不相逢。

Though the floating raft9 comes,

the floating raft goes,

we meet not.

星桥鹊驾，经年才见，

想离情、别恨难穷。

Magpies mount up

to bridge the stars.

A year has passed

since our last meeting.

Ruminating on our parting grief,

unable to exhaust its bitterness.

牵牛织女，莫是离中。

The Cowherd

and Weaver Maid –

are they separated still?

甚霎儿晴，霎儿雨，霎儿风。

Why is it that it’s clear one moment,

rainy one moment,

and windy still the next?

  1. ↑ 1129 falls during Li Qingzhao’s flight south following the Jin invasion and the fall of the Northern Song — a period of exile, displacement, and profound personal loss. The “parting grief” in the poem thus carries a literal dimension as well as an emotional one.
  2. ↑ 梧桐 (wútóng) — the Chinese parasol tree (Firmiana simplex), whose broad leaves fall early in autumn. In classical Chinese poetry, the sound of parasol leaves falling in the night is one of the defining images of loneliness and the melancholy of passing time.
  3. ↑ 浮槎 (fú chá) — a mythical raft or vessel said in Chinese legend to travel along the Milky Way between heaven and earth. Here Li Qingzhao uses it to suggest that even the cosmic passage between realms brings no reunion — the raft comes and goes, and still they do not meet.

Translations by Angela Xu. Original poems by Li Shangyin (李商隐, 813–858), Du Mu (杜牧, 803–852), Qin Guan (秦观, 1049–1100), and Li Qingzhao (李清照, 1084–c.1155).
