A Tale of Two Cities

Charles Dickens

I just finished reading A Tale of Two Cities by Charles Dickens. These past two years, I've really enjoyed reading classics in a way that I didn't growing up. Last year, I read The Count of Monte Cristo, which I surprisingly ended up loving. Perhaps I will write a post about it another time. This year, since I had the book on my shelf, I thought I'd make the attempt. It accompanied me through a trans-Pacific flight and a few dazed weeks after term.

Overall, I really enjoyed the book and found that it actually touches on similar themes as Monte Cristo, such as the cruelty of the social and legal system in France, the theme of resurrection, confronting a hidden past etc. I'd still rank the novel below Monte Cristo however — Dumas' masterpiece is truly exquisite in the distinctness and portrayal of each major and minor character, the lushness and diversity of settings, and the brilliant intricacy and unity embedded in a plot spanning a panorama of various corners of the Franco-Mediterranean world at the time. On the other hand, I found Two Cities to be less intricate (well it is a lot shorter…) but to have a more humane beating heart, more thematically focused, more pointed in its social-political criticism, more romantic in its character formation, and it ends with a more coherent answer to the question of meaning. This essay will focus primarily on A Tale of Two Cities, though I will make comparisons to Monte Cristo as it is the most recent novel I read of comparable literary standing.

I. Characters

Whereas Monte Cristo seemed to portray each character as in a detective story (which it sort of is), placing each character under a sharp light which throws into harsh relief each personality's unique combination of perversities, flaws, passions, torment, callous edges, and occasional softness — A Tale of Two Cities contains less in-depth character studies but rather uses characters primarily to fulfil certain roles or highlight certain themes. The most obvious example is Lucie Manette. She has no real flaws (actually, none in the Manette family seem to have any) and is portrayed as schematically as a good woman. Her husband Charles, despite his centrality to the plot, is essentially portrayed as a whole young man of good courage and character. How about his pain at separation from his homeland, life as an émigré, or his bonds with the folks in his home estate? He knew the injustice and corruption of the system and rejected it but seems to still have been removed from its anguish. Did his previous life or his own suffering not bring any contamination or damage to his character and hope?

Where I found the most compelling character portrayal is actually in the persons of Monsieur and Madame Defarge. The book opened with Madame Defarge solidly guarding her home knitting and Monsieur Defarge as a gentle man of middling profession with discernible decent human feelings in him. Perhaps one's first impression is that of a typical portrayal of a "good man" and "good woman." Then, through the course of the book, we watched them act heroically to aid the rescue of Dr. Manette, their long-held indignation at injustice and suffering boil over first in organised action and then descend into something increasingly unrecognisable, just like their country and city. By the end of the novel, Madame Defarge has transformed from a stolid faithful housewife into a vengeful spirit unrecognisably warped by tragedy and bloodlust — ultimately dying in an attempt to murder the daughter of the prisoner we first saw her help rescue. Similarly, Monsieur Defarge transformed into a man embedded in the revolutionary machine he had helped lead, which constrained and increasingly snuffed out those same decent and humane impulses which we first observed in him.

The final scene worth mentioning here is that of the Carmagnole. Where perversity and rough edges were not displayed in the central characters of the story, they were fully concentrated in descriptions of the mob. Dickens writes:

"It was so emphatically a fallen sport — a something, once innocent, delivered over to all devilry — a healthy pastime changed into a means of angering the blood, bewildering the senses, and steeling the heart. Such grace as was visible in it, made it the uglier, showing how warped and perverted all things good by nature were become." (Book 3:V)

So there — all dark and bright, noble and perverse — both extremes of the human character have been captured by Dickens in this novel.

II. Setting

The title, A Tale of Two Cities, highlights that the novel has two centres of gravity: Paris and London. The two are juxtaposed next to each other as foils: one feudal, the other professional; one a homeland, the other a new home of refuge. Paris is characterised by traditional industries (e.g. Defarge's wineshop) that are centrally embedded in political conflict while London is characterised by professional, merchant industries (e.g. Tellson's) based on capitalism with a focus on private and international ties. The City vibrates with characters devoted to their modern professions (e.g. Mr. Lorry), ambition (e.g. Stryver), dissipation (e.g. Sydney Carton), or unscrupulous business activities (e.g. Cruncher). Ironically, the two aspects the cities have in common are 1) deference to aristocratic rule (at least at first in Paris) and 2) the presence of linked criminal networks (e.g. Barsad and Cly).

I found most interesting the juxtaposition of the two spaces where public life was shown. In Paris, activity was oriented around the guillotine and the streets, indicating the dominance of mob politics. In London, public activity was oriented around the courts, indicating a more professional, capitalistic and institutional structure.

III. Themes

This book really shines in its integration of its core themes and they genuinely are sublime. The core theme is that of resurrection. Whereas Monte Cristo was built around its characters and plot, A Tale of Two Cities is built around its themes. The story begins with a description of the hives of activity and events in motion during the time and then proceeds to trace out a deeper pattern of life, death, and resurrection at work in and amidst the busy cacophony of the vast tapestry of human life. This particular narrative arc begins with the famous announcement: "Recalled to Life."

Dr. Manette's first life, which was on a promising path, was cut short by an unfortunate encounter with cruel aristocrats whose severe abuses of power had resulted in the ruin and death of one of their tenant families. One can say that Dr. Manette was effectively martyred by seeking judicial redress for what he had witnessed. The nature of his death was not physical but mental and emotional. He not only lost his profession, his family, his freedom, but also his identity, the truth of his life story. I think that is why he eventually lost his sanity — as the ultimate capstone in losing agency over his private mind. Today, we would call this severe trauma. In describing the Dr.'s reaction to his relapse, Miss Pross observes:

"Besides that, his loss of himself grew out of it. Not knowing how he lost himself, or how he recovered himself, he may never feel certain of not losing himself again. That alone wouldn't make the subject pleasant, I should think." (Book 2:6)

I note here a glimmer of René Descartes's "I think, therefore I am." Dr. Manette's release from prison was not only his personal resurrection but also the resurrection of a family, household, memory, legacy — and most importantly, I think — dignity and future. His trip back to Paris to redeem his son-in-law forces him to confront and overcome forces from the past which continue to obstruct the new life he is living.

Another smaller theme which I enjoyed was that things are not as they seem. The Defarges seemed harmless but turned out to be leaders of the mob. The Marquis seemed invincible but his life and estate was shortly vanquished. Sydney Carton seemed hopeless and helpless but turned out to be the keystone figure who precipitated the greatest hope and new life. The Marquis himself unwittingly articulates the theme:

"Hah! To the eye it is fair enough, here; but seen in its integrity, under the sky, and by the daylight, it is a crumbling tower of waste, mismanagement, extortion, debt, mortgage, oppression, hunger, nakedness, and suffering." (Book 2:9)

Finally, I found A Tale of Two Cities to be very thematically focused. Ultimately, the novel asks if and how simple goodness and decency can be preserved in a world gone awry. Dickens offers a coherent answer: sacrifice. It was not Dr. Manette's strength of will which cancelled the blood debt initiated by his denunciation letter from prison — it was the sacrifice of a stranger, Sydney Carton, who bore no blood nor business ties with the Manettes, only his freely given friendship and love. Whatever the form of sacrifice, the deeper principle is clear: the power to generate new life comes from giving.
