《关雎》

The Ospreys’ Call

Book of Songs (诗经)  ·  Zhou Dynasty, 11th–6th century BCE

“The Ospreys’ Call” — there’s no single accepted English translation of its Chinese title, so I’ve translated it two different ways — is the flagship poem of the classic of Chinese classics, the Book of Songs. The Book of Songs is a collection of 305 poems dating from the 11th–6th century BCE and is one of the foundations of the entire classical canon. “The Ospreys’ Call” is its first and probably most famous poem. It’s hard to understate the significance of this poem from a literary, philosophical, and sociological perspective as both representing the early core of Chinese civilization and influencing later generations.

“The Ospreys’ Call” is not an epic of battles or warriors but a simple love poem. Like a gentle love which unexpectedly appears and opens your eyes to the wonder and numinous quality of life, I think this poem captures that sense of treasuring the quiet quickening of living things, finally becoming fulfilled in a wedding finale between a worthy man and virtuous woman and its promise of the continuation of life. This poem accompanied me through several quiet afternoons at calligraphy club at school, so I thought I’d attempt the translation — despite the obvious challenge given the significance of this work — to communicate something of the beauty and peace of this poem.

The poem begins with two ospreys calling to each other on a river islet — presumably performing its mating call and dance. The poem then moves on to focus in on the graceful and virtuous (I’ve chosen the word “gentle” here) maiden ready for marriage. Then, the momentum builds through her first seeking, then plucking, then gathering in the plants floating on the water. The image is profound — just as the river nourishes life, the maiden prepares to move into the wonder of human union with its potential for giving new life and the maturation of the human personality. We see the young man seeking, yearning, frustrated, then finally courting the maiden. I made an interpretative choice in the final verse to use the pronoun “we” to indicate a communal wedding, where the two are properly wedded. This is a common interpretation by scholars, who also comment on the social philosophy aspect whereby social harmony begins with a harmonious marriage of the ruler. However, I will focus on the poetry in my translation and comments. These comments are my personal thoughts and interpretations.

In preparation for this translation, I re-read the Song of Songs, since I thought perhaps reading Biblical love poetry might put me in a more prepared mode to translate a love poem. Unfortunately, it turns out that the two are quite different, so Song of Songs wasn’t actually a great help in the translation. However, I thought I’d enumerate the differences here because I think it will help to clarify the aesthetic of “The Ospreys’ Call.”

The biggest difference between The Song of Songs and “The Osprey’s Call” is depiction and communication of passion. The Song of Songs uses language to bring us into an externalized field of sensual passion. Words fill up images and senses — dusk, flowers in bloom, figs ripe to bursting, fragrance of flowers, fruit, musk, blown by a thick wind on a balmy summer’s evening — we can almost see the pink and purple winds crisscross the sky. (Oh, the wonder of the phrase, “until the day breathes, and the shadows flee”!) On the other hand, “The Osprey’s Call” has quite a different aesthetic. Perhaps we sense a sunny day, sandy river banks, floating river vegetation, and perhaps even neighbors and children going about their business nearby. Instead of passion externalized, the poem almost leads us to a sense of passion internalized, hidden in plain sight on normal summer day.

Then, we go on to a description of the lovers. Both poems present seeking and yearning as key aspects of passion — though only on the male side in “The Ospreys’ Call” where we don’t see the maiden vocalize her yearning. Her role here may be inferred one of disciplined waiting and receiving, though we really don’t know what she’s thinking or feeling — though, I think we can infer from the general feeling that she very well reciprocates his love. Again, in the Song of Songs, the two lovers yearn and dream and their beloved becomes larger than life to them — each body part bursting with vividness, vitality, and life in various similes. Funnily, “She” seems inclined towards architecture comparisons, and “He” seems inclined towards battle-related and agriculture similes (e.g. He calls Her as “awesome as an army with banners”).

On the other hand, “The Ospreys’ Call” uses minimal language to describe the two characters. The maiden is only described as “graceful” and the man as a noble or virtuous man. We see him yearning, losing sleep, tossing and turning before he goes on to befriend the maiden with music. And his anxiety eventually turns to joy in the happy finale. But that’s it. Perhaps that’s the point. The man’s passion and longing for his beloved are restrained and noticeably unvocalized. Passion clearly exists but is primarily savored internally as a private affair. Confucius once famously commented that “‘The Osprey’s Call’ is joyful without overindulgence and sorrow without harmful overwhelm,” obviously approving of this balance of feeling and restraint.

I wonder what Confucius would have thought of The Song of Songs had he the chance to read it. Would he have thought that it was an overindulgence of passion or would he have found that it expressed proper feeling more fully?

《關雎》1

Coo-Coo Cry the Ospreys  ·  a.k.a. The Ospreys’ Call

Book of Songs (诗经)  ·  周南 (Zhou South)

關關雎鳩，在河之洲。

窈窕淑女，君子好逑。

Coo-coo cry the ospreys, across a river islet.2

Graceful is the gentle maiden,

a fitting mate for a noble man.

參差荇菜，左右流之。

窈窕淑女，寤寐求之。

Scattered3 are the water fringe leaves;

her hands4 trail through their drifting,

on the left and on the right.

Graceful is the gentle maiden;

waking and sleeping, he5 seeks after her.

求之不得，寤寐思服。

悠哉悠哉，輾轉反側。

Seeking her, but not finding her,

waking and sleeping, he yearns.

Oh endlessly, endlessly!

He tosses and turns.

參差荇菜，左右采之。

窈窕淑女，琴瑟友之。

Scattered are the water fringe leaves,

she plucks them to the left and to the right.

Graceful is the gentle maiden;

with zithers he befriends her.

參差荇菜，左右芼之。

窈窕淑女，鐘鼓樂之。

Scattered are the water fringe leaves,

she gathers them in from the left and from the right.

Graceful is the gentle maiden;

we delight over her with bells and drums.

  1. ↑ I usually use simplified Chinese in this blog, but because of how ancient and seminal this work is, I have copied the text here in traditional characters.
  2. ↑ The actual text says “on a river islet.” I have changed the wording to “across” to emphasize that it is a pair of birds calling to each other.
  3. ↑ Or uneven in height.
  4. ↑ No subjects are mentioned in the original text; inferred from context.
  5. ↑ All pronouns (he / she / we) are inferred in translation and not present in the original text.

Translation by Angela Xu. Original text from the Book of Songs (诗经), Zhou Dynasty, 11th–6th century BCE.
