On the Philosophy of Law

Reflections on Raymond Wacks's Philosophy of Law: A Very Short Introduction

I just finished reading Philosophy of Law: A Very Short Introduction by Raymond Wacks. Philosophy of law is something I've been curious about because I've realized that law is at the very heart of embodying truth amongst a group of people. Moses said that having God's law was a testament to how much God loved the Israeli people and the most important component of his blessing to them — which is to know his law. Through the law, God's righteousness is revealed.

The book documents the evolution of Western philosophy of law as the move from an anchoring in natural law with divine underpinnings through the Medieval Age, to a positivist understanding of law in the modern era, to Richard Dworkin's work on the moral integrity of law. The second half addresses application of economic and social theories to law and ends with a final section on more niche social theory critiques.

I. Natural Law

Aquinas's writings seemed to have formed the most thorough and developed articulation of natural law, which at its core stands the idea that law embodies the underlying command to do good and avoid evil. At this, my heart seemed to have come home: I am strongly reminded of Micah 6:8, which says "He has shown you O mortal, what is good. And what does the Lord require of you? To act justly and to love mercy and to walk humbly with your God." Wacks follows up with, "[w]e know intuitively what constitutes the good: it includes life, knowledge, procreation, society, and reasonable conduct" (pg. 5). This speaks to a vision of life suited for a settled way of being which prioritizes peace with neighbors and flourishing together. To me, the statement embodied is that "life is good, and God called his creation very good."

II. Legal Positivism

The next major development in legal philosophy occurred in the 1700s — legal positivism, which instead of prescribing what law should be, recommends that it be abstracted for study as its own entity. I think this is an amazing development because it sets the technical stage for the law to develop a "soul" of its own. Jeremy Bentham gave an early glimpse of this vision by his notion of a single "complete" law whereby each component law defines the "narrowest species" of a certain kind of offence. On the other hand, John Austin emphasizes the "lynchpin" holding together the law, which is the command of the sovereign. Thus, he identifies the practical efficacy and integrity of law to be the political force which enforces it — which today is not necessarily a sovereign but a constitutional legal body. However, probably as a reflection of the social and political conditions of the time, law was described as a system of commands from the sovereign with somewhat of a coercive undertone.

The apex of legal positivism was the work of H.L.A. Hart, who used linguistics to technically define and expand the scope, or mechanics, of a body of law. He gives further technical definition to the idea of law as an independent entity by bestowing upon it a most fundamental rule of recognition — endogenous to the system — that determines whether or not a rule is recognized as a rule. He also gives the law a system of endogenous rules that allows it to self-regulate and evolve, stipulating the conditions under which these are recognized and enacted. Although H.L.A. Hart is called a legal positivist, I think his conception of the law already has begun to grow a soul — which he thinks is the internal social fabric of a community that acknowledges a level of equality, mutual vulnerability, and interdependence.

A brief aside: Wacks has a section on Hans Kelsen and his conception that the body of law is a system of norms whose authority is predicated on the "basic norm." His view essentially boils down to a realist understanding of political realities. The "basic norm" is essentially a recognition of the current political force as "legitimate," and the purpose of law is solely the monopolization of force. I didn't find his conception particularly original — it seemed to restate the ideas of classical legal positivists with even more emphasis on political domination as the basis for rule by law. I did, however, find a stronger imprint of continental Constitutional law in his work, versus the common law tradition whose influence was stronger in Hart.

III. Dworkin and the Moral Integrity of Law

Next, Wacks moved on to Richard Dworkin's theories — and I was starstruck. This philosopher's work was so brilliant, with enough intellectual scope to bridge a deep historical philosophical gap and shot through with enough moral force and beauty to reshape modern jurisprudence. I can't help but admire and feel grateful for such a brilliant mind put to work in such a public field.

Essentially, Dworkin puts the moral soul back into law, which had developed greater mechanical and technical breadth over the positivist era. Instead of a direct link to divine revelation, this moral soul is characterized by recognition of the integrity of the body of law and the plumbing of a finer alignment within its driving principles and policies. Laws are not formed as an evolving system of rules with an "internal script" regulating their evolution, per Hart, but rather must be painstakingly worked out from the embodied principles already extant within. This understanding of the law as carrying values at its driving heart allows him to advocate for greater weight to legal interpretation rather than pure mechanical semantics.

This quote by Dworkin was particularly striking:

"[L]aw as integrity accepts law and legal rights wholeheartedly… It supposes that law's constraints benefit society not just by providing predictability or procedural fairness, or in some other instrumental way, but by securing a kind of equality among citizens that makes their community more genuine and improves its moral justification for exercising the political power that it does… It argues that rights and responsibilities flow from past decisions and so count as legal, not just when they are explicit in these decisions but also when they follow from the principles of personal and political morality the explicit decisions presuppose by way of justification."

Put another way, law is the social and political embodiment of justice. Personally, this deepens my conviction that moral foundations matter. Psalm 89 says "Righteousness and justice are the foundation of your throne; steadfast love and faithfulness go before you." It sounds intuitive to me that greater measures of righteousness and justice enacted make the associative community more genuine and ruling powers more legitimate. Dworkin puts this idea straight back into the heart of law as what ought to be its driving force. Hence, his theory of rights holds that the right to equal concern and respect is fundamental to human dignity and fair society, and that equality is assigned primacy over liberty.

IV. Law, Society, and Economy

The book goes on to address various discussions of law in the context of economics and society. A few points stuck out to me. Émile Durkheim points out that as society advances and becomes more complex and interdependent, law can act as the mechanism to promote social cohesion. Max Weber emphasized the dichotomy between the civil-bureaucratic model of legal administration versus a charismatic or traditional model. Karl Marx emphasized the "class instrumentalism" side of the law — that it was used as an instrument to the benefit of the elite against the masses.

Overall, I walk away from this book with a deeper appreciation of the principles at stake at the heart of law, as well as a deeper appreciation for its historical development — observing the changes in perception of each successive philosopher and surmising something of the social and political conditions that contextualized their thinking.
