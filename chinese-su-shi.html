Two Poems by Su Shi

Su Shi (Su Dongpo)  ·  Song Dynasty

So far in my (shallow) exploration of Chinese poetry, my favorite poet has been Su Shi. Su Dongpo was a Song Dynasty statesman, master poet and essayist as well as renowned in calligraphy, painting, and architecture among others — a true Renaissance Man in the category of Leonardo da Vinci.

Among the classical poetry pieces, much of which focuses on themes like political exile, ambition and frustration, grief and loneliness of war, I find Su Dongpo’s poetry to be comparably more playful, creative, and illuminatingly brilliant. He was known for greatly expanding the range, subject matter, and expressive capacity of contemporary ci poetry and brought it to a level on par with the more recognized shi as a form of serious poetry. The ci form was set to established melodic chants and embodied a graceful, flowing form of beauty emblematic of the Song. Previously, it was a genre connected with banquet and courtesan culture — a feminine and private world.

I have also taken some liberties with translation in certain parts as to make the poem more intelligible in English and to communicate some nuances of meaning.

《水调歌头 · 明月几时有》

"When Did the Bright Moon Come to Be"

丙辰中秋，欢饮达旦，大醉，作此篇，兼怀子由。

Mid-Autumn, 1076. I joyfully feasted until dawn and composed this poem while greatly drunk. Longing for my brother, ZiYou.

明月几时有？把酒问青天。

“When did the bright moon come to be?”

I raise my goblet1 to the azure sky.

不知天上宫阙，今夕是何年。

In the moon’s palace above2,

I know not which year it is tonight.

我欲乘风归去，又恐琼楼玉宇，高处不胜寒。

I wish to ride the wind and go there

but am worried that in her jeweled palaces and jadeite pavilions,

I shall not be able to bear the chill.

起舞弄清影，何似在人间。

I dance and play with my limpid silhouette —

indeed3 where else is like the human realm?!

转朱阁，低绮户，照无眠。

Turning about the vermillion tower,

peeking beneath the carved lattices4,

shining on the sleepless —

不应有恨，何事长向别时圆？

“Surely you have no cause to bear a grudge —

But why must you ever wax full when we are parted?”

人有悲欢离合，月有阴晴圆缺，此事古难全。

People have sorrow, joy, parting, and meeting;

the moon darkens, brightens, waxes and wanes in its turn —

since of old, these things have rarely been whole.

但愿人长久，千里共婵娟。

But I wish that we each may remain long

and together delight in the same moon across a thousand miles.

《江城子 · 密州出猎》

“Hunting at Mi Zhou”

老夫聊发少年狂，

左牵黄，右擎苍。

锦帽貂裘，千骑卷平冈。

This old man suddenly had a fit of youthful madness —

a leashed yellow hound on my left,

a hawk borne high on my right.

In a brocade hat and sable coat,

behind me5, a thousand riders roll across the open plain.

为报倾城随太守，

亲射虎，看孙郎。

To repay this city for following their governor,

I shall shoot a tiger myself —

they shall watch the young lord Sun6!

酒酣胸胆尚开张。

鬓微霜，又何妨！

Wine-flushed, my heart and courage nevertheless inflamed and emboldened7 —

a little gray at the temples, but what does it matter!

持节云中，何日遣冯唐？

Oh, when will my lord dispatch Feng Tang to Yunzhong with the imperial command?8

会挽雕弓如满月，

西北望，射天狼。

Then, drawing my carven bow full as the moon,

I shall turn to the Northwest

and shoot the Wolf of Heaven.9

  1. ↑ Assumed wine/alcohol beverage.
  2. ↑ The word “moon’s” is inserted for clarity in this line — absent from the original text.
  3. ↑ “Indeed” here is placed for emphasis and is not contained in the original text.
  4. ↑ The original text says “hanging low upon the carved lattices.” I took some liberties with the translation here to emphasize the personification of the moon and the playfulness of the tone.
  5. ↑ This phrase is inserted; absent in the original text.
  6. ↑ The founding king of Wu during the Three Kingdoms era, who once famously killed a tiger on a hunt.
  7. ↑ The word-for-word translation is “wine-flushed, my chest and gallbladder nevertheless open wide.”
  8. ↑ This is a reference to the story of Wei Shang during the Han Dynasty, who despite being an able, loyal, and generous commander, was dismissed and penalized for a clerical error. Feng Tang took note of his situation and remonstrated with the Emperor on his behalf. The Emperor eventually sent Feng Tang with the imperial seal to pardon and reinstate Wei Shang in Yunzhong. Here, Su Shi is expressing his aggrievement and frustration at not being given more important responsibilities within the court.
  9. ↑ The last line expresses his ambition at accomplishing a great work despite his relative age — here referring to the contemporary incursions by nomadic peoples into the Song’s Northwest border. The Sirius star, or Heavenly Wolf, was said to be the star governing war, invasions, and raiding. Su Dongpo refers to his desire to contribute and accomplish a work of renown in the protection of the Song frontier.

Translation by Angela Xu. Original poems by Su Shi (苏轼, 1037–1101), Song Dynasty.
