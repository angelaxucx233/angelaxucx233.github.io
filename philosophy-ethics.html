On Ethics

Reflections on Simon Blackburn's Ethics: A Very Short Introduction

I recently read Simon Blackburn's Ethics: A Very Short Introduction. First of all, I enjoyed the author's voice. The author addresses an old and complex topic with clarity, thoughtfulness, and later passion and critique. Through his work, I thought I discerned a voice who despite being embedded deeply in an academic conversation, still rang with a sincerity that says: "Ethics matter for human life and I care about it deeply."

Ethics is the branch of philosophy that studies morality. But I think its scope is actually broader — it is a study of what makes for a human life well lived — that is a life lived with meaning, good conduct, and in the old Greek word, fulfilling eudaimonia.

The paragraphs below cover some of the thoughts that this reading inspired in me as well as some of my own responses. My insights and perspectives have been formed by my own life experience as well as my understanding of the Christian Scriptures.

I. Foundations

The book discussed the philosophical foundation of the discipline of modern Western ethics as the evolution from Reason (with capital R, as revealed by a Supreme Being) to a more empirical approach with individual passions in the driver's seat and reason acting as a descriptive scaffolding — i.e. reason only helps us to understand the outer landscape of our world. Other foundations for ethical principles include the moral instinct of a collective humanity, some belief that true human flourishing requires true justice, an existential explanation which gives that one should act as if the maxim of your action were to become by your will a universal law of nature (which actually sort of ties back to Reason with capital R), and communal survival benefits deriving from emergence of shared norms (which to me seems to be the empirical explanation).

My gut response to this is that these approaches feel quite different from the East Asian tradition of ethics. The modern Western tradition seems to be founded on rationalism and empiricism, moving in direct response — or rebellion — to medieval traditions rooted in religion and yet at the same time, unable to shake off its legacy and existential influence. In this way, I wonder if modern Western philosophical development can be thought of as one overarching dialectic between Supreme Reason and Will vs individual reason and will. I am by no means an expert on these topics. Some of these observations are intuitions based on my understanding, which is limited.

I wonder perhaps the reason why Western philosophy developed this way is that it seemed to have been deeply founded on the idea that God is a person and has intrinsic qualities such as reason, passion, and agency. Hence, maybe that's also why in philosophies such as existentialism, there is such a focus on fully deploying these capabilities — because in doing so, we become more fully a person, and also are able to access some of the qualities of personhood that have been divinely imprinted or bear the touch of divine energies. Further, there seems to be a fascination in some quarters with the exercise of human agency to shape the natural world and even its underlying moral structures. That's probably existentialism at its zenith.

Hence, perhaps that is also why the Western conception of freedom and especially later political thought seem to have the paradigm that a legitimate political system involves a legitimate and logical way to adjudicate the exercise of individual passions and wills. Because there is such a strong presupposition in human agency, conflict of the domination of will is presumed to be a permanent characteristic of human interactions. After all, modern Western philosophy was formed in the context of intense religious and political conflict and in reaction to a medieval religious hierarchy that seemed to impose a perhaps oppressive form of "God's will." Hence, if even God's will could be violently imposed, then naturally it seems that people will follow suit. Out of this context, I wonder if it could be said that one angle to looking at modern Western political philosophy is that its goal is legitimate co-existence with some sort of mechanism for when and under what conditions can one's political will be imposed.

On another note, I think the development of the idea of personhood is also evident in self-help books like the very popular Boundaries by Dr. Henry Cloud and Dr. John Townsend. Underlying the concept of boundaries is the idea that the more we allow for space to individuate, the more space it creates for true love and flourishing. Certainly, the development of the concept of personhood has been immensely valuable and foundational for fields like modern psychology, and also genuinely practical and helpful when applied to life and relationships. However, at the same time, I do feel that things like society and communal dynamics are also real — they carry history and habits (both good and bad), moral instincts, aesthetic taste, identity-level instincts and values which can persist for a long time. I suppose what all this points to is a communal participation in shared meaning, which I think is an important part of being human.

On the other hand, while I don't have a background in Chinese philosophy (I do have a book waiting on my bookshelf), I do have a little sense for it through readings I did in Classical Chinese class. I think Chinese ethics through the Confucian branch is actually founded on seeking alignment with Wisdom (with a capital W) rather than Reason as in the case of French Rationalism. Honestly, encountering some of the ideas of René Descartes and seeing the impact of rationalism on French aesthetics and its approach towards mathematics makes me think that Reason has indeed led many people to touch and experience something divine. But whereas the rationalist ethical approach has tended towards discovery and support of universal principles, the wisdom-driven approach assumes the existence of a universal presence or principle (Dao) but instead focuses on its application in specific situations because it acknowledges that the Dao can never be fully understood or plumbed.

From the perspective of someone with faith in the Christian scriptures, the idea of Wisdom with a capital W is in line with the concept of Divine or Lady Wisdom as described in Proverbs 8. From a biblical perspective, Lady Wisdom is the personification of divine wisdom (and could be a pre-New Testament incarnation of Jesus Christ) whose characteristics include its eternal nature (Proverbs 8:22–31), self-evidence to mankind (8:1–4), alignment with justice (8:8), inestimable value (8:10–11), founded upon knowledge of God (Supreme Being) (8:13), embedded in the underlying moral structures of the world (8:15–17), and connected to prosperity (8:18–21). The Chinese concept of the Dao bears many similarities to Lady Wisdom, including its embeddedness in the course of Life, its moral foundation, and its ability to be sought by man — but differs in emphasis on its unsearchable nature and sometimes presents the Dao as a dialectic with a darker side as well. My knowledge of Chinese philosophy is also very limited — this is an observation from a simplified understanding of many philosophers. Perhaps I'll have to write a follow-up post after doing some more readings!

In political philosophy, I think this contributes to a very deep assumption that human communal life has a fundamentally moral foundation. Hence, political and social models are meant to reflect something about the moral nature of communal relations. I don't find this assumption in modern Western philosophy as much. I think this starting orientation also causes a more natural emphasis on social and cultural rights. Perhaps the driving question isn't so much — how can we protect the exercise of individual will, but rather, how does a person participate rightly in communal life, which in turn specifies the limits of their social boundaries. I wonder if in East Asian social and political discourse or in its public consciousness, the sanctity of the right of proper participation may trump the sanctity of exercising autonomy. The question is difficult for me to answer without a much deeper understanding of contemporary social and political issues in Asia.

However, on the exercise of political will, the canonical texts of Confucius and Mencius provide very little practical guidance for any sort of mechanism for the balance of powers, or system for adjudicating different political wills — the essence of guidance is that righteous governance increases in prosperity and strength while unrighteous governance can't be called governance at all. As for political will, well, it is assumed that the king's power reigns supreme and forms the basis for all political authority. Considering that Confucius was active at a time of a proliferation of localized kingdoms and city-states across the wide spectrum of moral track records, this reflects a deep-seated assumption on the cosmic order very close to the idea of the divine right of kings in early modern Europe. And, if your time on earth unhappily happens to fall under the era of a wicked king, well your best options are to become a hermit or teacher and wisely bide your time rather than be caught in the treachery of a wicked era.

In my reflection upon the evolution of modern Western ethics, I observe its ambition as well as the very real way it encourages greater individuation and diversity of developmental paths while protecting the dignity of each. In my reflection on the foundation of East Asian ethics, I also recognize the reality and importance of a moral foundation to communal life as well as the reality of the communal nature of dignity and a wise and generous life.

Finally, as the history of human society continues to unfurl through time, I also wonder what philosophical innovations or frameworks could help to make sense of and contribute to the development of our current era with its new social, political, and technological challenges.

II. Topics in Ethics

Paternalism

Blackburn goes on to cover paternalism as a topic in ethics. Following on to my discussion in the previous section, perhaps the best practical expression of the Western philosophical evolution is a mature adult who is grounded in his or her ethical values but also respects others by not interfering or restraining them. Underlying this is a healthy sort of confidence in an individual's judgment of their own interests and stewardship of their own life while remaining at the same time healthily tethered to common sense and universal values.

I find this topic has practical applications for the formation of children. Children suffer when they don't have any sort of guidance at all on moral sensibilities, values, and good behavior. At the same time, children also suffer from the type of parenting which denies them the exercise of their own judgment and will in forms that are too restrictive, imposing, or interfering. This type of parenting can potentially injure the child's developing personhood by negating the validity of the child's developing preferences, judgment, and agency.

The Importance of Time

In a later section, Blackburn remarked on the importance of gradualism in evaluating topics like abortion. That is to say, the age of the fetus does matter; a one-week old fertilized cell is different than a fetus in the first trimester, the third trimester, and a fully developed baby. This made me reflect on the importance of time in human affairs in general. The course of things have a beginning, middle and end, a point where paths branch off, wander in separate directions, and realign.

I suppose time matters because humans are organic materials which come to being, grow, mature, disintegrate, and die in a process that is fundamentally grounded in time. Hence, if ethics is morality that is aligned with humanity, then surely time will need to play a prominent role in our consideration.

For example, does ethical standards change with a person's age? Our laws generally indicate a belief in a threshold of responsibility. However, at the same time, how many people do we know are very responsible for their age and others lag far behind. Suppose a man becomes a father at age 19 and again at age 45. Would we judge — nay require — different standards at the different ages?

Even in the realm of ethics-relevant interactions, time has a big impact. A lie told at the beginning of a business negotiation is different than one told three rounds in and even more different than one told after a deal is signed. Presumably, the same action at the different times have different degrees of consequence and require different levels of moral courage.
