On Knowledge

Reflections on Jennifer Nagel's Knowledge: A Very Short Introduction

When I encountered philosophy during my undergraduate studies, the subject felt confusing, a bit difficult to grasp, and content-wise, sometimes unfounded. My coursework had focused mostly on social and political philosophy. Recently, I picked up Knowledge: A Very Short Introduction by Jennifer Nagel as a hobby read (well, it's short!) and am finding this foray into philosophy more comprehensible and penetrating than the last. I am coming to realize that philosophy is fundamental to our individual and collective lives. Metaphysical philosophy (of which the study of knowledge is a related branch) is the channel through which we perceive truth, which is then built into social and political philosophies with massive impact on human societies. A truthful foundation is necessary because it is the most lasting when held up to the test of reality.

On a personal level, I've found this exercise to be very grounding. As someone who often doubts my own perception and judgment, reading about mechanisms and boundaries in search of knowledge — which is confident belief in something true — is helping me relax into judgment and knowledge as fundamental human capacities.

Below, I've written down some thoughts on the ideas presented within the book, which includes the development of the concept of knowledge (scepticism, rationalism, empiricism), theories of knowledge (classical vs causal, internal vs external) and knowledge transmission, reception, and confidence (testimonies, contextualism, invariantism) and the intuition of knowledge itself.

I. Knowledge can be proven false

The time-tested framework for describing the concept of knowledge is the link between a knower and a truth. Hence, if something is true but not known by someone, it has not come into the realm of knowledge — and if a person has confident belief in a fact that is not, in fact, true, that belief itself is not knowledge. In the classical analysis of knowledge, the knower also needs a reasonably justified belief in the true fact to be counted as knowledge. In this framework, a justified belief linked to a false fact would not be counted as knowledge.

While acknowledging the need to avoid relativism in anchoring, I think the absolute link between knowledge and truth is not justified and causes logical problems later on.

It is historically obvious that it is possible to have knowledge that turns out to be false. Before Galileo provided evidence for heliocentrism, most learned men knew that the sun revolved around the earth, just as we now know that Mt. Everest is the highest mountain on earth. Via external theories of knowledge, this fact was transmitted via legitimate channels and became the building block for a network of communal theories.

We also now know that that knowledge, in fact, was false. When its falsity was revealed, it transformed science and the trajectory of social development in Europe. The classical theory would call this false belief rather than knowledge. I disagree with classical theory's absolute link between truth and knowledge, simply because knowledge aims to reflect truth but, given the ungraspable nature of truth, will always fall short. However, falling short does not make knowledge wholly illegitimate — which is my answer to scepticism. I propose a loose definition of the term knowledge which also includes justified beliefs which have been inducted into a communally recognized structure of knowledge.

II. Perspicuity of Truth

So far, we see in the philosophy of knowledge the exercise of knowers extending themselves to further knowledge. However, I think it would be clarifying to make some claims from the opposite angle, on the nature of truth. We can make the claim that truth is perspicuous: that it is clear, accessible and makes sense to the average person. That is, truth wants to be known — and hence, knowledge and our intuitive recognition of knowledge, is legitimate.

This claim provides support for some of the modern answers to scepticism, which uses common sense claims (G.E. Moore) or Inference to Best Explanation (Bertrand Russell) in lieu of a negative proof to all alternatives.

The current debate over scepticism seems to have gone in the route of seeking increasingly roundabout scenarios where what seems to be actually is not. For example, a deceptive demon is sending us vivid and coherent images to give us the impression that we are living in a physical world when in reality we are brains in a vat.

However, by making the claim that truth is perspicuous, we can claim that we have standing positive reason that our perception of reality is in line with truth, unless there is a reason otherwise.

III. Multiple dimensions of knowledge

Theories of knowledge so far have focused on rationality, sensory experience, and differentiation as the main channels of knowledge to the self. René Descartes focuses on innate, clear, and distinct ideas — such as abstract mathematical knowledge — as entirely trustworthy, accessed and discerned by pure rationality. On the other hand, John Locke emphasizes powers of differentiation between objects, logic, and sensory data as the basis for knowledge.

However, I propose that there are actually more channels through which we "know" something. What if in addition to the rational, differentiation, and sensory channels we have additional capabilities or "antennas" by which we access and intuit knowledge? For example, moral intuition can also be called a form of knowledge. At the end of the movie Rashomon, one man saw another man steal a valuable kimono and amulet that an abandoned baby was wrapped in, and violently exclaimed, "What you did is an evil thing!" There was something honest in him which knew what had happened was morally wrong.

Further, there is sometimes a sense that when coming into knowledge about something, there is an umph in us which agrees with the fact we came into contact with it. Further research into the intuition of knowledge has been acknowledged to be one area that can be helpful in expanding our understanding (pg. 115).

The presence of this moral and instinctual response — perhaps multi-dimensional forms of recognition — can be added to rational and sensual responses to truth. I propose adding a form of recognition as an additional vector in the study of knowledge. It does not mean that truth must be recognizable to all people, for we know that is not the case (e.g. heliocentrism). But I think this approach can add value as the mechanism between truth and knowledge, which can be applied to the Tracking Theory of Knowledge and the method of belief formation.

Note: After writing this section, I discovered that a concept called virtue epistemology covers many of the ideas explored here.

IV. Communal knowledge structures

In analyzing knowledge from testimony, philosophers have formed concepts of global reductionism (if no special warning signs, standing positive reason to believe testimonies at large) and local reductionism (needing specific positive reasons to accept the testimony at hand). While both are useful for everyday accrual of relevant knowledge, I think one addition which could increase our understanding of knowledge is formulating a concept of knowledge structures: interlinked facts that describe reality as is.

This innovation could help clarify some of the issues with reductionism. On one hand, it would acknowledge that there is a structure of global knowledge one can tap into. On the other, it would also acknowledge that the structure is made of knowledge, which could be false — which is why local reductionism methods are still needed.

V. Inheritance of knowledge

One aspect of knowledge which follows the idea of communal knowledge structures is the inheritance of knowledge through time and the evolution of knowledge structures. So far, the philosophical methods laid out in this book treat knowledge as access to truth at a certain point in time, as if it is immovable. As we see from Section I, knowledge may be proven false, and thus knowledge structures evolve over time. Information might be forgotten or remembered again; false facts may be inserted and then rectified.

VI. Problem of Deceit

In defining and building a body of knowledge, I think the trickiest aspect is the problem of deceit. Humans are creatures with capacity for deceit. Hence, people can deceive others, and also deceive ourselves. Deceit includes outright lying as well as slanting knowledge to the benefit of specific interests or prior beliefs. Deceit does not include genuine partial ignorance.

It is important to note that the topics covered so far in this reflection do not deal with the problem of deceit. I think deceit deserves special attention in constructing sound theories of knowledge.

Overall, I am grateful I came into contact with some of these ideas through reading this book. These ideas helped me to reflect on the concept of knowledge and also helped me to gain confidence in trusting knowledge and reality.
